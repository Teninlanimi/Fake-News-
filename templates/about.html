📰 About this app

No jargon — just what this thing is, what it's good for, and where it fails.

The one-sentence version

Paste in a news article or headline, and the app tells you whether it reads like real news or fake news — with a confidence percentage. A spell-checker, but for the style of news writing.

How did it learn to do that?

Imagine someone who spent weeks doing nothing but reading about 6,300 news articles — roughly half real, half fake — until they internalized the way each type tends to be written: calm and sourced on one side, dramatic and shouty on the other. After all that reading, they'd guess which is which just from the writing style — the same way you can often spot a tabloid headline at a glance without checking any facts.

That's exactly what this program did. It's a pattern-spotter for words and writing style.

What it's genuinely useful for

  • A rough gut-check on article-style text — if a piece of "news" reads like tabloid junk, the app will usually say so (~96% accurate within its world).
  • Learning how AI actually works — this project shows the full honest arc: build it, measure it, deploy it… then deliberately stress-test it to find where it breaks.
  • An automatic quality watchdog — every update rebuilds the model and re-runs its "exams"; if a change makes it dumber, the update is rejected automatically.

Where it breaks down

We deliberately tested it on short political claims fact-checked by PolitiFact — something it had never seen. The result:

49% accuracy — a coin flip. Random guessing does better.

Why? Because it never learned "what's true." It learned "what professional news writing looks like." A false but calmly-worded claim fooled it completely; a true but dramatic-sounding one got flagged as fake.

Other honest limits

  • It doesn't know facts. It can't check whether unemployment is really 3.9% — it only knows what words tend to appear next to each other.
  • It can be confidently wrong. Outside its comfort zone it doesn't say "I don't know" — it confidently gives a wrong answer.
  • It lives in a small world. It studied one collection of US news articles from the mid-2010s, in English.
  • Clever fakes can pass. A polished fake sails through; a sloppy true story gets flagged. It judges the wrapping paper, not the gift.

The takeaway

Think of this app as a writing-style inspector — a decent one, within its lane. It is not a truth machine, a lie detector, or a fact-checker. Knowing exactly where it can't help you is the most useful thing it offers.
